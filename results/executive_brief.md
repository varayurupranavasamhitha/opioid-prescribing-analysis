**Headline:** Opioid prescribing is concentrated in a small group of doctors, and an optimised visit plan is expected to deliver the biggest reduction from Tennessee's $90,000 budget.

**Key insights**
- **A few doctors write most opioid prescriptions.** Among 804,678 Medicare prescribers in 2016, the top 10% accounted for 69.0% of all opioid claims.
- **A doctor's specialty matters far more than location.** Specialty has a large effect on opioid prescribing (effect size 0.268), while region has only a tiny one (0.032). Orthopedic surgeons' median opioid rate is 47.4%, versus 4.0% for family doctors.
- **Prescribing is already falling.** Opioids dropped from 6.45% of all prescriptions in 2013 to 5.98% in 2016, and the same 525,852 doctors lowered their rate by 1.4 points on average.
- **Smarter targeting gets more from the same money.** The optimised plan is expected to avoid 86,211 opioid claims: 10.4% more than visiting the highest-volume prescribers, and 6.3 times more than visiting those with the highest opioid rates.
- **Fairness is affordable.** Guaranteeing West Tennessee at least 25% of visits costs only 0.3% of the expected impact.

**Recommendation**
Adopt the optimised visit plan, which focuses mainly on primary-care doctors, and start with a pilot that measures how much doctors actually change after a visit. The plan's advantage held between 4.3% and 13.4% when the key assumption was varied, but the responsiveness figures remain assumptions until the pilot confirms them.

**Main limitation:** CMS hides opioid counts of 1-10 for privacy, so low-volume prescribers are under-represented.
